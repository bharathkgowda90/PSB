Pre-Primary

Pre-School, Pre-KG, LKG and UKG. Four years that take a child from their first morning away from home to being ready for Class 1.

Leaving your child for the first time

For most families this is the hardest part, and it is worth saying plainly: children cry in the first week. Almost all of them stop. Teachers here expect it and are not flustered by it.

You are welcome to stay for the first few mornings until your child settles. If a child is not settling after a couple of weeks, the school will talk to you about it rather than let it drift.

The four years are not the same

Parents often think of the pre-primary years as one long block. They are not. What the school is trying to do changes each year.

Pre-School and Pre-KG

Settling in

The work of these two years is being comfortable away from home. Playing alongside other children, sitting for a short activity, eating and drinking without help, telling an adult when something is wrong, and managing the toilet independently.

There is very little formal learning, and that is deliberate. A child who is happy to come to school learns quickly later. A child pushed too early often does not.

LKG and UKG

Getting ready for Class 1

Now the early academic work begins: recognising and forming letters and numbers, listening to and retelling stories, counting and simple addition, colours, shapes and patterns, and spoken English built through songs, rhymes and daily conversation.

By the end of UKG a child should be able to sit and concentrate for a full lesson, hold a pencil properly, and follow instructions given in English.

A typical morning

  • Assembly and prayer
  • Circle time: talking, songs and rhymes, mostly in English
  • An activity at the table: writing, colouring, counting or craft
  • Snack and toilet break, with an adult helping the youngest
  • Free play, indoors or in the playground
  • A story before going home
To be confirmed

Actual timings, whether pre-primary is a half day or full day, snack and meal arrangements, and nap arrangements for the youngest children.

Care and supervision

Young children need looking after as much as teaching. What matters to most parents is who is watching their child, how many children each adult is responsible for, what happens if a child falls ill during the day, and how the child is handed over at home time.

To be supplied

Number of children per class and per adult, whether a helper is present alongside the teacher, first aid arrangements, illness policy, and the handover procedure at home time including who is allowed to collect a child.

What you can do at home

  • Keep bedtime and waking time steady, especially in the first month
  • Practise the toilet, washing hands and eating independently before school starts
  • Talk about school as an ordinary, expected thing rather than a reward or a threat
  • Say goodbye clearly and leave; a long, uncertain goodbye makes it harder
  • Read or tell a story every day, in any language
